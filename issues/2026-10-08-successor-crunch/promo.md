## Substack Notes（5本）

**Note 1 — 数字ひとつ**
85.5%.
That's the share of Japan's successor-shortage bankruptcies this year (April–September) where the owner had died or was in poor health.
The usual story is "owners can't find buyers." The data says many run out of time first.
[LINK]

**Note 2 — 表の切り出し**
Japan's government keeps a register of M&A advisers. I counted how many people they actually have on M&A:
• None: 24.6%
• 1–2 people: 50.2%
• 3 or more: 25.2%
Registered ≠ resourced. Ask who will work on your deal.

**Note 3 — 誤解の補正**
A common line in English: "Japan has hundreds of thousands of profitable companies waiting to be sold."
What the Japanese data shows: many never get listed. In 94.6% of this year's successor-shortage bankruptcies, the company was liquidated. There was nothing left to buy.

**Note 4 — 売り手側の視点**
Something you won't read in a teaser: when a one-person-run Japanese company loses its owner, things can unravel in days. Staff sell equipment. A spouse inherits a business they never ran.
If you're buying, the owner's health is part of due diligence.

**Note 5 — 締切カレンダー**
A date for anyone watching Japanese small-business deal flow: December 31, 2027.
That's when Japan's special tax break for handing company shares to a family successor runs out, unless it's replaced.
Some owners will have to decide: hand it down, or sell.

## LinkedIn（2本）

**LinkedIn 1 — 買い手（サーチファンド・PE）向け**
If you're looking at Japanese small businesses, here's a number worth knowing.

Between April and September 2026, Tokyo Shoko Research counted 263 bankruptcies where the lack of a successor was a factor — a record for that period, up 23.4% year on year.

In 85.5% of them, the owner had died or was in poor health.

The English-language story about Japan's succession problem is mostly about supply: so many companies, so few heirs. The Japanese data adds a timing problem. A lot of these firms never reach a broker or a platform. The owner's health gives out first, and the company is liquidated.

For buyers, that changes the playbook. Waiting for listings means seeing only the companies that made it to market in time. Owner age, owner health and whether there's a capable No. 2 belong in your first conversation, not your last.

I wrote this week's issue of The Japan Handover on this, plus what Japan's register of M&A advisers really looks like. Link in comments.

**LinkedIn 2 — 仲介・アドバイザーの選び方**
Japan's SME Agency keeps a register of M&A advisers and intermediaries. Being on it is often presented as a mark of trust.

I went through the agency's latest breakdown (September 24, 2026):
– 3,362 registrants, 37 fewer than in March despite 75 new additions
– 24.6% report no staff dedicated to M&A
– 50.2% have one or two
– 25.2% have three or more

Registration means a firm has pledged to follow the government's M&A guidelines. It doesn't tell you whether it has the people to run a careful process, or whose side it's on when it's paid by both buyer and seller.

Three questions I'd ask any Japanese intermediary before engaging:
1. How many people will work on this deal?
2. What is your fee schedule? (Registered firms publish it.)
3. Are you also being paid by the other side?
