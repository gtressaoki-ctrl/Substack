*This is the first issue of The Japan Handover. Each week, I read Japanese sources on small-business succession and tell you what they mean for a buyer.*

In Japan, a small company with no successor rarely ends in a sale. More often, the owner dies or falls ill first, and this week's data shows that happening faster than at any point on record. If you are waiting for these companies to show up on a deal platform, you are often arriving after it is over.

## 1. Successor-shortage bankruptcies hit a record

Tokyo Shoko Research (TSR), one of Japan's two big credit-research firms, counted 263 bankruptcies between April and September 2026 in which the lack of a successor was a factor. That is up 23.4% from a year earlier, the first increase in three years, and the highest for that six-month period on record. The previous high was 222, in the same period of 2023. TSR only counts cases with liabilities of ¥10 million or more. ([TSR](https://www.tsr-net.co.jp/data/detail/1203300_1527.html), in Japanese)

The causes are what matter. In 120 cases the owner had died, and in 105 the owner was in poor health. Together, that is 85.5% of the total.

These are tiny firms: 68.4% had capital under ¥10 million. And 94.6% ended in liquidation-type bankruptcy (*hasan*), not a restructuring. In other words, there was no business left to buy.

**What it means for a buyer:** the window between "the owner is still well enough to negotiate" and "the company is gone" can be short. The supply you see on platforms is the part that made it to market in time.

**What I'd check:** the owner's age and health, and whether there is a second-in-command who already runs daily operations.

**From the owner's side:** in a company that one person ran alone, things come apart quickly when that person is gone. I have heard of employees selling off the company's machinery, and of spouses who inherited the business but could not hold it together.

## 2. Japan's register of M&A advisers is shrinking

Japan's Small and Medium Enterprise Agency (SME Agency) keeps a register of M&A advisers and intermediaries. To be listed, a firm declares that it will follow the agency's M&A Guidelines for SMEs.

As of September 24, 2026, there were 3,362 registered firms and individuals. That is 37 fewer than on March 9, even though 75 new ones were added in the latest round. ([SME Agency, September](https://www.chusho.meti.go.jp/zaimu/shoukei/2026/260924m_and_a.html); [March breakdown](https://www.chusho.meti.go.jp/zaimu/shoukei/2026/260309m_and_a02.pdf), in Japanese)

**What it means for a buyer:** being registered is a minimum bar, not a quality signal. It means the firm has promised to follow the guidelines. It does not tell you whether it has the people to run a careful process.

**From the owner's side:** many Japanese owners treat cold pitches from intermediaries as something to note, not act on. And many intermediaries mainly want the deal to close, since that is when they get paid.

**What I'd check:** how many people will actually work on your deal, the fee schedule (registered firms publish theirs in the agency's database), and whether the firm is being paid by both buyer and seller.

## 3. A tax deadline for family succession stays at the end of 2027

Japan has a special tax measure that lets a family successor defer gift and inheritance tax on the company's shares. To use it, the owner must file a succession plan. That filing deadline was March 31, 2026; it has now been extended to September 30, 2027.

The deadline that matters more did not move. The gift or inheritance itself must happen by December 31, 2027. What replaces the measure after that is expected to be decided in the next round of tax reform. ([Osaka Prefecture](https://www.pref.osaka.lg.jp/o110050/keieishien/keieisyoukeienkatuka/tokureisyo.html), in Japanese)

**What it means for a buyer (my guess, not data):** some owners may be pushed to choose between handing the company to a child and selling it during 2027.

**From the owner's side:** I suspect the tax break matters less than people assume. Many owners of small regional firms see handing a struggling business to their children as a burden they would not wish on them. They would rather sell and leave their children money.

**What I'd watch:** Japan's annual tax reform outline, usually released in December, for what happens after 2027.

## One number

**85.5%** — the share of successor-shortage bankruptcies caused by the owner's death or poor health.
