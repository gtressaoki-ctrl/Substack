# Where Japan's successor gap is widest — and why that isn't where the deals are

*Three Japanese datasets, read side by side: who has no successor, who is closing, and who is failing.*

---

If you are looking to buy a small business in Japan, the first real decision is not which company. It is which sector to spend your next three months on.

The obvious way to choose is to find the sector where the most owners have no successor. By that measure, the answer is construction: it ranks highest in Teikoku Databank's 2025 survey of about 270,000 companies, at 57.3%.

But "no successor" is not the same as "for sale." Many of those owners never sell. They close, or the company fails when they fall ill. So in this issue I put three Japanese datasets side by side — who has no successor, who is closing down, and who is going bankrupt for lack of one — and read them as a buyer would.

The short version: construction and services show pressure on every measure, manufacturing shows the least, and the most useful signal is where those measures disagree.

## 1. Who has no successor

Japan has two big credit-research firms, and both publish an annual survey on this. They do not agree on the level.

Teikoku Databank (TDB) puts the national rate at 50.1% for 2025, down 2.0 points and improving for the seventh year in a row. ([TDB, November 21, 2025](https://www.tdb.co.jp/report/economic/20251121-successor25y/), in Japanese)

Tokyo Shoko Research (TSR), using a different set of about 169,000 companies, puts it at 62.60% and rising. ([TSR, November 10, 2025](https://www.tsr-net.co.jp/data/detail/1201982_1527.html), in Japanese)

The two firms use different samples and sector groupings, so the levels are not comparable. What matters for a buyer is the ranking within each survey — and on that, they point the same way.

[画像を挿入：chart-sector-no-successor.png]

In TDB's data, every one of its eight sectors is now below 60% for the first time since 2011. Construction is highest at 57.3%, down from a peak of 71.4% in 2018. Manufacturing is lowest at 42.4%.

TSR breaks things down differently. Its highest sector is information and communications, at 77.06%. Construction (63.69%) and "services and others" (67.28%) both rose from the previous year.

TDB's finer breakdown shows where the gap is sharpest. The highest sub-sectors in 2025 were:

- car and bicycle dealers: 62.3%
- specialist construction trades (such as house-building subcontractors): 61.3%
- medical practices: 59.0%
- car repair and parking: 58.8%
- professional services: 58.6%

The lowest was finance and insurance, at 31.4%.

## 2. Who is closing instead of selling

A company with no successor has three exits: someone takes it over, it closes, or it fails. The second is by far the most common.

TSR counted 67,210 voluntary closures and dissolutions in 2025, a record for the third year running and up 7.2%. By sector, the largest numbers were:

- services and others: 21,961 (up 9.2%)
- construction: 10,283 (up 9.5%)
- retail: 7,903 (up 9.7%)

The fastest growth was in information and communications, up 15.2% to 4,189. ([TSR, January 9, 2026](https://www.tsr-net.co.jp/data/detail/1202284_1527.html), in Japanese)

Two more numbers from the same survey matter for buyers. First, 52.8% of the companies that closed were profitable in their last reported year. Second, the average owner of a closing company was 74.9 years old, and more than a third were in their eighties.

In other words, about half of the companies that shut down last year were not failing businesses. They were profitable firms whose owners stopped.

## 3. Who is failing for lack of a successor

The third exit is bankruptcy. TSR counted 461 successor-shortage bankruptcies in Japan's fiscal year to March 2026, the highest since its records began in fiscal 2013. In 84.8% of cases, the owner had died or was in poor health. ([TSR, April 8, 2026](https://www.tsr-net.co.jp/data/detail/1202733_1527.html), in Japanese)

By sector:

- services and others: 119 (up 29.3%)
- construction: 96 (down 9.4%)
- manufacturing: 74 (down 3.8%)

These are small numbers next to closures, and 94.7% ended in liquidation. But the direction is worth watching. In services, failures are rising fast.

Three readings stand out.

**Construction is the clearest case of supply outrunning succession.** It has the highest share of owners without a successor, its closures are rising, and its rate in TSR's survey went up, not down. One practical point for buyers: a Japanese construction company's operating license stays with the company if you buy its shares. If you buy only its business or assets instead, the license does not move automatically; since October 2020, it can be carried over only with prior approval from the authorities. ([Tokyo Metropolitan Government guide](https://www.toshiseibi.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/documents/d/toshiseibi/pdf_kenchiku_kensetsu_pdf_2024_R06_kensetu_tebiki_04), in Japanese)

**Services is where companies leave the market fastest, in every way.** It leads closures and successor-shortage bankruptcies, and its bankruptcies are growing quickest. But "services" covers restaurants, care homes, consultancies and much else. Treat it as a list of sub-sectors, not one market.

**Manufacturing has the smallest gap, and that is not an accident.** TDB notes that worries about supply chains, starting with the car industry, have brought concentrated support for succession in manufacturing. For a foreign buyer, that likely means better-prepared sellers but more competition from Japanese strategic buyers.

## 5. Size, place and lender

Sector is one cut. TDB's survey offers three more that matter for where you look.

**Size.** Among large companies, 24.9% have no successor. Among small and medium-sized ones it is 51.2%, and among the smallest firms it is 57.3%. Since 2023, the rate for large firms fell 5.5 points; for the smallest, only 2.9. Succession planning is reaching small firms last.

**Place.** The rate ranges from 33.9% in Mie Prefecture to 73.7% in Akita, the only prefecture above 70% and the only one to rise three years in a row. Next highest are Shimane (64.2%), Hokkaido (63.6%), Kochi (63.2%) and Tottori (62.7%) — mostly rural, ageing regions.

**Lender.** This is the cut I find most useful. Among small and medium-sized companies whose main bank is one of Japan's megabanks, 44.2% have no successor. For government-affiliated lenders, it is 42.3%. But for companies that bank mainly with a *shinkin* (a local co-operative bank), it is 57.9%, and for credit co-operatives, 56.2%.

**What it means for a buyer:** the owners with the least succession planning are concentrated in small, rural firms that bank with local co-operative lenders. Those lenders know these owners better than any platform does. They are also the slowest channel for a foreign buyer to reach, which is part of why the gap persists.

## 6. Who is taking over instead

One more number shows how quickly the old default is fading. Among Japanese companies that changed their head in 2025, TDB found (preliminary figures):

- promotion of an executive or employee from inside: 36.1%
- family succession: 32.3%
- M&A and similar: 20.6%
- an outside hire: 7.6%

For the first time, insiders overtook family. A non-family successor is no longer unusual in Japan.

That is good news for buyers, with one caution. TSR notes that many owners still avoid handing their business to an outside party. An owner who is open to an insider is not automatically open to a stranger, let alone a foreign one.

## 7. What I'd do with this

If I were starting a search tomorrow, this is how I would use the table. It is a way to frame questions, not a recommendation.

1. **Start with sub-sectors, not sectors.** "Construction" or "services" is too broad. Specialist trades, car dealers and repair shops, and professional services all sit above 58% in TDB's data.
2. **Look for profitable firms with old owners, before they close.** Half of last year's closures were profitable. The useful window is before the owner decides to stop.
3. **Learn how regional lenders work.** The firms with the least succession planning bank with local co-operative lenders, not with megabanks or platforms.
4. **Ask how the deal will be structured early.** In construction, a share purchase keeps the license; a business transfer needs prior approval.
5. **Check whether the business falls into a sector the government screens.** Under Japan's Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act, certain designated sectors tied to national security and public order require prior notification for foreign investment. Confirm this with a lawyer before you get far. ([Ministry of Finance](https://www.mof.go.jp/policy/international_policy/gaitame_kawase/index.html), in Japanese)
6. **Expect competition where the gap is smallest.** Manufacturing looks easier, but that is partly because others got there first.

## Method and caveats

- TDB and TSR use different samples, definitions and sector groupings. I compare rankings within each survey, never levels across them.
- TSR publishes most sector figures in charts; I used only numbers stated in the text of each release. Blanks are blanks.
- Closures and bankruptcies are counted by different TSR surveys over different periods (calendar 2025 and the fiscal year to March 2026).
